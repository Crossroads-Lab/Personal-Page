# ViewPlus Technologies

## Have you worked on accessibility technology?

I have. From September 2006 to June 2007 I was a Computer Vision Researcher with Oregon State University and ViewPlus Technologies in Corvallis, building assistive technology for blind users.

## What did you build at ViewPlus?

A real-time system for braille document registration and finger tracking, so the software could follow where a blind reader's finger was on a physical braille page and know which part of the document they were touching.

The constraint I'm proudest of is that it ran on an off-the-shelf, low-resolution webcam. No specialised sensor, no custom rig. That was deliberate — assistive technology that needs expensive hardware doesn't reach the people who need it.

## What did that project teach you?

That the hard part of applied computer vision is usually the deployment constraint, not the algorithm. Tracking a finger is tractable. Tracking a finger reliably, in real time, on whatever cheap camera someone already owns, is a different problem — and it's the one that decides whether the work is useful or just publishable.

I've carried that bias since: build for the hardware people actually have.

## Is there a line from that project to what you did later?

A direct one. ViewPlus is the first time I pointed this technology at someone whose daily life was harder than it needed to be, and it's the reason founding Heali felt obvious rather than like a career change.

One was helping blind readers navigate a page. The other was helping people with chronic conditions work out what to eat. Thirteen years apart, same instinct — and that instinct is what I mean when I talk about AI for good, rather than it being a label I reached for afterwards.
