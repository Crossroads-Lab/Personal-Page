# Running distributed teams

## Have you managed remote or distributed teams?

Yes — at both Heali and iScribeHealth I ran teams spread across four continents. That wasn't a pandemic arrangement I adapted to; it was how those organisations were built, and it's most of my management experience rather than an exception to it.

## How do you handle accountability when nobody is in the room?

The honest answer is that distributed work doesn't break accountability, it breaks the informal signals managers lean on instead of accountability. In an office you can tell someone is stuck by how they're sitting. Across eight time zones you cannot, so you have to make the thing you were inferring explicit.

That means being specific about what done looks like, and being specific early — not because people need policing, but because ambiguity that would be resolved by a two-minute hallway conversation can quietly cost a distributed team a day.

Transparency does most of the work. If the state of things is visible to everyone without anyone having to ask, you don't need to manufacture check-ins to find out.

## Doesn't leading by example fall apart when you're not co-located?

It changes shape. You can't demonstrate standards by sitting next to someone, so it shows up in what you're visibly willing to do: taking the awkward timezone yourself rather than always making the same region take it, being the one who writes the thing down, being reachable when it's genuinely hard rather than only in your own working hours.

The night our services went dark at iScribe, the most useful thing I did was get food delivered to two engineers' homes so they could last the night. That's the distributed version of being in the trenches — you can't stand next to someone, so you work out what actually helps from where you are and you do that.

## What's hardest about it?

That the people who struggle remotely are the ones least likely to say so. Isolation doesn't announce itself in a status update, and someone drifting is indistinguishable from someone quietly getting on with it until the gap is large.

So I'd rather over-invest in whether people are alright than in whether work is progressing. The work is usually the easier thing to see.
