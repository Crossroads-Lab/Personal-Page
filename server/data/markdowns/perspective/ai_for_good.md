# AI for good

## What do you believe AI should be used for?

Helping people who are up against something they didn't choose. That's not a slogan I adopted late — it's the thread running through the work I've picked when I had a choice.

In 2006 I built real-time braille registration and finger tracking so blind readers could be followed on a physical page, and I built it to run on a cheap off-the-shelf webcam because assistive technology that needs expensive hardware doesn't reach anyone. In 2019 I co-founded Heali to help people with chronic conditions — diabetes, IBS, cardiovascular disease — eat in a way that reduced their symptoms, across 200+ medical conditions.

Thirteen years apart, same instinct. Take a capability that exists and point it at someone whose daily life is harder than it needs to be.

## Why does that matter to you specifically?

Because the technology is indifferent and the choice of target isn't.

The maths that recommends the next video you'll watch is close kin to the maths that predicts what someone with a chronic illness should eat. The models don't care. Somebody decides which problem gets the attention, and that decision is where the ethics actually live — not in the architecture.

Spending a decade making recommendation systems better at holding attention taught me how much capability was being pointed at fairly trivial ends.

## Is "AI for good" not just marketing?

Often, yes. It's a cheap thing to claim and an expensive thing to do, and the gap between the two is where most of it sits.

The test I'd apply is whether the good outcome and the business outcome are the same outcome. At Heali, a person whose symptoms improved was also a person who kept using the product — those pulled in the same direction. Where they pull apart, the mission language usually loses quietly, and nobody announces it.

So I'd rather be judged on which problems I chose to work on than on what I called them.
