# How I see myself

## How would your toughest critic describe you?

Too realistic.

I'd rather own that than dress it up. The same instinct that makes me useful when a plan is about to meet the world makes me the person who punctures the room's optimism, and not everyone wants that at the moment I deliver it. I've learned to time it better. I haven't stopped doing it, because the alternative is finding out later at greater cost.

## What's the most misunderstood aspect of your leadership style?

The thing people most often misread is that when I'm doing the work alongside my team, I'm doing their job for them. People read it as not trusting them, or as a manager who can't let go.

It's the opposite. Leading by example is teaching — mentoring, passing down the unspoken knowledge that never makes it into documentation, and then getting out of the way. It is how you empower someone, not how you supervise them.

Micromanaging is standing over the work. Leading by example is being in it with them and then leaving them to it. From a distance those can look similar for about a week. After that the difference is obvious: one of them produces people who need you, and the other produces people who don't.

## How do you evaluate your own performance when the metrics are subjective?

I redefine them so they aren't. There's no point running on subjectivity.

If the only available measure of whether I'm doing well is somebody's impression, that's not a measurement problem, it's an unfinished definition. Almost anything that matters has an observable proxy — cycle time, retention, how long a decision takes to get made, how often the same problem comes back. Find it and commit to it before you need it to say something flattering.

Subjective assessment ends up measuring who is most comfortable arguing for themselves. I'd rather be judged by something that can tell me I'm wrong.

## How do you measure whether a culture is healthy?

Six things, and I check them by watching rather than surveying: respect, kindness, empathy, whether people are actually empowered, fairness, and transparency.

Culture isn't what a company says it values. It's what happens when someone makes a mistake, when someone junior disagrees with someone senior, and when there's bad news to deliver. Watch those three moments and you have your answer in a week.

## What's the toughest decision you've made in the last six months?

The biggest one in the last six months hasn't been technical at all. It's been which vehicle to build in — my own company, or one already in motion — and it's a decision I make repeatedly, not once.

(If you're after the hardest *technical* call, that's a different question and I have a longer answer to it: staying on Azure at iScribeHealth.)

What settles it isn't security. It's alignment: whether the mission is one I believe is worth the next few years, whether the culture is one I'd want to be measured by, what the company is actually trying to change, and whether the role is one where I can move the needle rather than describe it. When those line up, I take the opportunity, and I take it with gratitude — that isn't the consolation prize, it's the better vehicle for the same goal.

Changing something at scale has many paths and founding a company is only one of them. I've done the founder version and the inside-a-company version, and the work I'm proudest of has come from both — the turnaround at iScribeHealth as much as anything I started myself. What I won't do is choose the safe thing because it's safe, or the hard thing because it's hard.
