# The book

## Have you written a book? Are you a published author?

Yes — I'm a co-author of *The AI Transformation: Thriving Within Civilization's Next Big Disruption*, a multi-author collection on how AI is reshaping work and society and what people can actually do about it. It's an Amazon bestseller. You can find it on Amazon at amazon.com/dp/1968318747, ISBN 9781968318741.

## What's your chapter about? What did you contribute?

My chapter is "AI: The Reality Beyond The Fantasy", and the argument is that we're watching for the wrong failure.

Hollywood conditioned us to expect the machine turning on us. The real failure mode is slower and organisational: a team so dependent on model outputs that nobody keeps the habit of questioning them, a bad decision made because a system sounded confident, a company that automates through a workforce reduction and finds two years later that the institutional memory it needed to course-correct has left.

The comparison I use is Disney's pivot away from hand-drawn animation in the early 2000s. They chased the efficiency of CGI and nearly extinguished the hand-crafted warmth that made people care about Disney in the first place. Optimising so hard for efficiency that you lose track of what the efficiency was meant to serve.

It opens as a day in the life of a fictional executive — the notification that lands before the alarm, the hollow report at 10:17, the afternoon spent fixing what was generated in minutes — because that's how this actually arrives. Not as an event. As a Tuesday.

To be clear about whose day that is: it's invented, written in second person, and it isn't mine. It's a composite of the leaders I keep having this conversation with.

The whole chapter is in *The AI Transformation*, on Amazon at amazon.com/dp/1968318747.

## Is the chapter anti-AI?

No, and I'd push back on that reading. I spent twenty years building these systems.

It's against the over-promise, not the technology. The gains are real but narrow, and they're routinely quoted outside the conditions they were measured in. The chapter lays out where AI genuinely delivers — Walmart's routing, Klarna's ticket handling, narrow problems with clean data — alongside the research on security, privacy, cognitive cost and environmental impact that vendor decks leave out.

The closing question is the one I think leaders are avoiding: in times of change, what are we actually trying to preserve?

## Where else have you published?

Most of my writing is research rather than trade. I've authored 30+ patents and publications with 2,200+ citations, from the PhD work on video segmentation and event recognition through to patents filed at Google, Amazon, and Snap. I was also publication chair for CVPR.

## What research does your chapter cite? What's the evidence behind it?

More than people expect from a chapter that reads like a story, and that's deliberate — you can't argue against over-promising without numbers.

There's a well-known MIT study in *Science* showing large productivity gains from ChatGPT, and I quote it. I also quote the caveat that almost never travels with it, and a second MIT study from 2025 where experienced developers were measurably slower while being certain they'd been faster. Alongside that: the Microsoft and Carnegie Mellon survey on how little critical thinking gets applied to AI output, and an EEG study that went looking for the effect in the brain rather than in a questionnaire.

The sources are all cited in the chapter so you can check them yourself, which is rather the point. It's on Amazon at amazon.com/dp/1968318747.

## What does your chapter say about privacy and AI?

That most people have no idea what they're handing over, and that the well-known corporate leak everyone half-remembers is the ordinary case rather than the exotic one.

The number that stopped me while writing it was from an analysis of 1.6 million workers: a meaningful share of what employees paste into consumer AI tools is confidential, and almost none of them have read the terms that decide where it goes.

The rule I give is one line — treat anything pasted into an AI tool as potentially shareable outside your organisation — and then the chapter spends its pages on the guardrails that follow from it. amazon.com/dp/1968318747.

## Does the chapter cover AI's environmental cost?

It does, with the numbers, because it belongs in the same honest accounting as everything else.

The part that surprises people isn't the training run, it's what comes after: every query costs electricity, cooling and water, and the water figure for one major provider's data centres is large enough to change how you think about "just ask the model". The chapter has the sources.

I'm not arguing anyone should stop. I'm arguing that deploying AI at scale is an environmental and operational decision as much as a technical one, and the decks selling it rarely say so.

## Where does the chapter say AI genuinely works?

Where the rules are clear and the data matches the real world. Hand it an inhuman volume of similar cases and it moves.

I walk through two cases in detail — a logistics overhaul that took real money and real emissions out, and a support operation that cut resolution time by most of it — and both have the same shape: a narrow problem, clean data, a measurable before and after. The counterexample is in the same breath. Hand it your general infrastructure with poor supervision and you end up with a house held together by termites.

The full cases, with the figures, are in the chapter: amazon.com/dp/1968318747.

## What should a leader actually do about it? What does the chapter recommend?

Five things, and none of them is "adopt faster".

Test productivity claims the way an engineer tests a benchmark, under real conditions with real people. Decide your data boundaries before somebody else discovers them. Keep humans in the loop and mean it, because a workforce that stops questioning outputs eventually stops noticing errors. Start narrow, prove value, repeat — routing, classifying, reconciling; rarely glamorous, always measurable. And hold accountability steady through the change.

The chapter gives each of those its own section and the reasoning behind it. The line I'd keep if I could keep one: a small focused team scaling mindfully with AI has an undeniable advantage over a large dragging department.

## Why is the chapter written as a day in someone's life?

Because that's how this actually arrives. Not as an event — as a Tuesday.

It follows an invented executive, written as "you", from a notification that reaches them before their alarm does, through a report at 10:17 that is correct, organised, grammatically sound and completely hollow, to a dinner table where four people are in the same room and three are somewhere else. Then to the next morning, which is the only part that's different.

An argument made in statistics lets a reader agree and change nothing. A day they recognise is harder to put down. That executive is not me and not a case study — it's a composite of the conversations I keep having with leaders who are lying awake with some version of this and none of whom mention it in the morning.

## What's the hardest moment in the chapter?

The one where the executive walks the floor in the morning — and it's worth saying again that this is a character, not a diary entry.

The team have read the same articles he woke up to, and what's in their eyes is neither panic nor ease. They've sat through the meetings where leadership said "augmentation" and "evolution" with the careful diction of people coached by HR, and they're smart enough to hear what isn't being said. He wants to reassure them and can't make the promise that reassurance requires.

There's a second one later, with a red pen over that hollow report, where he realises he isn't angry at the people who produced it — and that anger would have been easier. How that lands is the part I'd rather you read than have summarised.

## Is there a Matrix reference in it?

There is, late at night, and it's doing more work than a reference usually does.

Red pill, blue pill. The character remembers knowing instantly which one he'd have taken — of course the red pill, of course you want to see how deep it goes. He was the kind of person who found discomfort energising rather than depleting.

The chapter puts it there for the doubt that comes next. That's the cost it's actually about: not the machine turning on you, but the appetite for hard truths going quietly first. amazon.com/dp/1968318747.
