# The book

## Have you written a book? Are you a published author?

Yes — I'm a co-author of *The AI Transformation: Thriving Within Civilization's Next Big Disruption*, a multi-author collection on how AI is reshaping work and society and what people can actually do about it. It's an Amazon bestseller. You can find it on Amazon at amazon.com/dp/1968318747, ISBN 9781968318741.

## What's your chapter about? What did you contribute?

My chapter is "AI: The Reality Beyond The Fantasy", and the argument is that we're watching for the wrong failure.

Hollywood conditioned us to expect the machine turning on us. The real failure mode is slower and organisational: a team so dependent on model outputs that nobody keeps the habit of questioning them, a bad decision made because a system sounded confident, a company that automates through a workforce reduction and finds two years later that the institutional memory it needed to course-correct has left.

The comparison I use is Disney's pivot away from hand-drawn animation in the early 2000s. They chased the efficiency of CGI and nearly extinguished the hand-crafted warmth that made people care about Disney in the first place. Optimising so hard for efficiency that you lose track of what the efficiency was meant to serve.

It opens as a day in the life of an executive — the 5:58am notification, the hollow report at 10:17, the two hours spent fixing forty minutes of generated code — because that's how this actually arrives. Not as an event. As a Tuesday.

## Is the chapter anti-AI?

No, and I'd push back on that reading. I spent twenty years building these systems.

It's against the over-promise, not the technology. The gains are real but narrow, and they're routinely quoted outside the conditions they were measured in. The chapter lays out where AI genuinely delivers — Walmart's routing, Klarna's ticket handling, narrow problems with clean data — alongside the research on security, privacy, cognitive cost and environmental impact that vendor decks leave out.

The closing question is the one I think leaders are avoiding: in times of change, what are we actually trying to preserve?

## Where else have you published?

Most of my writing is research rather than trade. I've authored 30+ patents and publications with 2,200+ citations, from the PhD work on video segmentation and event recognition through to patents filed at Google, Amazon, and Snap. I was also publication chair for CVPR.

## What research does your chapter cite? What's the evidence behind it?

More than people expect from a chapter that reads like a story, and deliberately so — the argument is about over-promising, which you cannot make without numbers.

On productivity: a 2023 MIT study in *Science* by Noy and Zhang found ChatGPT cut the time college-educated professionals took by 40% and raised output quality 18%. That one gets quoted everywhere, and the caveat almost never travels with it — the tasks excluded real workplace context. A 2025 MIT study put it more bluntly: experienced developers using AI wrote code faster but took 19% longer on the overall task, while believing the tool had made them at least 20% faster.

On cognitive cost: Microsoft and Carnegie Mellon surveyed 319 knowledge workers in real workplace use and found that on 40% of tasks they applied no critical thinking to the output at all — and that higher confidence in the tool correlated with *less* thinking. MIT's Media Lab went further and measured it with EEG during writing tasks: LLM assistance produced the weakest neural coupling of any condition they tested, weaker than using a search engine, weaker than working alone.

On security: Georgetown's CSET found code generation models frequently output insecure code, and a 2024 peer-reviewed literature review found roughly 40% of Copilot-generated programs contained vulnerabilities, with the same insecure patterns recurring across languages and tools.

## What does your chapter say about privacy and AI?

That most people have no idea what they are handing over.

The example I use is Samsung: engineers used ChatGPT to debug code and summarise internal meetings, and confidential source code went into OpenAI's systems. That is not an exotic failure, it is a Tuesday. Cyberhaven analysed 1.6 million workers and found 11% of what employees paste into consumer AI tools is confidential — and most of them have never read the terms of service that decide where it ends up.

The rule I give in the chapter is blunt on purpose: treat anything pasted into an AI tool as potentially shareable outside your organisation. Then set actual guardrails — what data can be used, where it can be processed, who stays accountable for the result. Trust in these systems starts with being clear about boundaries, not with being enthusiastic about capabilities.

## Does the chapter cover AI's environmental cost?

It does, because it belongs in the same honest accounting as everything else.

Training GPT-3 emitted roughly 550 tonnes of CO2. And the real cost is not the training run, it is every query after it — electricity, cooling, water. Google reported over 6 billion gallons of water used in its data centres in 2023, driven in part by AI workloads.

My point isn't that this should stop anyone. It's that deploying AI at scale is an environmental and operational decision, not only a technical one, and the decks that sell it rarely say so.

## Where does the chapter say AI genuinely works?

Where the rules are clear and the data matches the real world. Hand it an inhuman volume of similar cases and it moves fast.

Walmart overhauled truck routing in 2023 — letting an algorithm obsess over load optimisation cut $75 million in costs and 72 million pounds of CO2. A narrow problem met with clean data. Klarna's assistant was handling two-thirds of their support tickets across 23 markets in early 2024, and average resolution went from eleven minutes to under two, on routine things: order status, payment questions, return policies.

The counterexample is in the same paragraph. Hand it your general infrastructure with poor supervision and you end up with a house held together by termites. I also say plainly that claims about agentic AI are still too fresh to measure long-term.

## What should a leader actually do about it? What does the chapter recommend?

Five things, and none of them are "adopt faster".

Test productivity claims like an engineer tests a benchmark — under real conditions, with real people doing real work. If the gain holds, scale it. If it doesn't, adjust without embarrassment. Treat anything pasted into a tool as potentially public, and decide the boundaries before someone else discovers them. Keep humans in the loop and mean it: a workforce that stops questioning outputs eventually stops noticing errors. Start small, prove value, repeat — routing trucks, classifying tickets, reconciling invoices, work that is rarely glamorous and always measurable. And hold accountability and trust steady through the change, which means protecting the capabilities that make people valuable while machines take the rest.

The line I'd keep if I could keep only one: a small focused team scaling mindfully with AI has an undeniable advantage over a large dragging department.

## Why is the chapter written as a day in someone's life?

Because that is how this actually arrives. Not as an event — as a Tuesday.

It runs from 5:58 a.m., when a notification gets to you before your alarm does, to 11:32 p.m., and then to 6:15 the next morning. In between: a report at 10:17 that is correct, organised, grammatically sound and completely hollow. Two hours after lunch fixing an AI-generated module that a mid-level developer would have written from scratch in forty minutes. A dinner table where four people are in the same room and three are somewhere else entirely.

An argument made in statistics lets a reader agree with it and change nothing. A day they recognise is harder to put down. The executive in it is not a case study, it's the composite of conversations I keep having with leaders who are lying awake with some version of this and none of whom talk about it in the morning.

## What's the hardest moment in the chapter?

Two, and they are the same moment seen from different sides.

The first is walking the floor in the morning. Your people have read the same articles you woke up to, and what you see in their eyes is neither panic nor ease. They have sat through the meetings where leadership said "augmentation" and "evolution" with the careful diction of people coached by HR, and they are smart enough to hear what is not being said. You want to reassure them and you cannot make the promise that reassurance requires, because investors want margin and the maths keeps pointing somewhere uncomfortable.

The second is at 10:17, red pen over a hollow report, realising you are not angry at the people who produced it — and that anger would have been easier. They felt the pressure to ship faster, the tool was there, and you are the one who pushed it on them.

## What's the Matrix reference about?

Late in the day the executive is on the couch, scrolling past a thriller about AI surveillance and a documentary about what recommendation algorithms did to a generation of teenagers, and notices those films used to feel thrilling because the future in them was far enough away to visit and leave. That distance has closed.

Then Morpheus and the two pills surfaces. When you first saw that image you knew instantly which one you'd take — of course the red pill, of course you want to see how deep it goes. You were the kind of person who stayed curious, who found discomfort energising rather than depleting.

The point of putting it there is the doubt that follows. Cognitively overloaded, you are no longer sure. That is the cost the chapter is actually about: not that the machine turns on you, but that the appetite for hard truths quietly goes first.
