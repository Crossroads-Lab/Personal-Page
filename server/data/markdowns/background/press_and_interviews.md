# Press and interviews

## What was the RE•WORK interview about?

That was the Deep Learning Summit in Toronto, October 2018 — about twenty-two minutes with RE•WORK, on the record.

The subject was my work at Snap Research: computer vision, NLP, new optimization frameworks, and teaching machines to learn how to learn. That last one is the thread the rest hang off, and it's why the conversation was worth having rather than a tour of features.

The concrete version of that work: I was directing a 50+ person R&D organization across 40+ concurrent projects, shipping research into products serving 188M+ daily active users. I'd expanded the group into 10+ research domains — face analysis, NLP, scene understanding, augmented reality, video analysis. Privacy-preserving friend recommendations revived 14% of inactive users and lifted daily engagement 7%. Bitmoji chat recommendations drove an 8% engagement increase. Sentiment-based ticket sorting used NLP to raise support productivity 71% by resolution volume.

The video is on YouTube and it's linked from this page.

## Why does "learning how to learn" matter to you?

Because the alternative doesn't scale, and at Snap the scale was the whole problem.

Ten research domains and forty concurrent projects is more than any one group can hand-tune. What you need is systems and a team that get better at the next problem because of the last one, rather than starting from zero each time. That's as true of the organisation as it is of the models.

It's also the honest version of what research leadership is. You are not there to have the ideas. You are there to build the thing that keeps producing them after you've moved on.

## What's the Oregon State article about?

It's a College of Engineering piece titled "Serendipity and Oregon State led to William Brendel's career in AI", and the title is doing real work — serendipity is the accurate word.

I came to Oregon State in 2004 as a French exchange student, and I picked it because it was the closest school on my shortlist to the ocean. I wanted to surf. I meant to stay a year and stayed seven. What I actually wanted to do was computer graphics — building technology for animation and visual effects studios. That was the dream, not machine learning.

AI was what was left. I did not choose the field so much as end up in it, and the article is the university's version of that story, which is a generous reading of a series of accidents.

I like that it's public. A career told backwards always sounds deliberate, and mine wasn't.
