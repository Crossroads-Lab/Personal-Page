# Being told I wouldn't make it

## What's the biggest obstacle you've had to get past?

Being told, during my engineering studies in France, that I would never make a good engineer — if I even passed the bar at all. And separately, that I would never make it into management.

That was the assessment from the people responsible for assessing me. I was not a strong student at the time, so on paper they had a case.

What I understand now, and didn't then, is how circular that is. You tell someone they won't make it, their grades reflect it, and the grades confirm the original judgement. I was holding up my end of that bargain perfectly.

So I decided to leave. If this was the ceiling here, I'd go somewhere nobody had decided what I was yet. That meant the United States.

## How did you actually get to the US?

Badly, and twice.

My grades weren't good enough for a place, so I went on the wait list. The first year, I didn't make the cut.

Rather than wait around, I took a year at Infineon in Munich — an application engineering job, building test platforms and hardware drivers. I didn't speak a word of German when I arrived. It was the first time I'd bet on being able to figure something out after committing to it rather than before.

Second attempt, same outcome: wait list again, and the same people who had told me I wouldn't make it were the ones standing between me and the place.

Then another student who was due to go fell ill at the last minute, and I took the spot.

## So you got there by luck?

Partly, yes, and I'm not going to pretend otherwise. Somebody got sick and I got a seat. That's not merit.

But I'd been on the wait list twice, spent a year in a country whose language I didn't speak to stay in the game, and was ready to move the week the call came. Luck arrives for a lot of people. What matters is whether you're in a position to take it when it does.

## Did you prove them wrong?

I try not to frame it that way, though I'd be lying if I said it never crosses my mind.

I was told I wouldn't make a good engineer. I finished a PhD, authored 30+ patents and publications, and shipped systems used by hundreds of millions of people at Google, Amazon and Snap.

I was told I'd never make it into management. At Snap I directed a 50+ person R&D organisation across 40+ projects.

The more useful lesson isn't that they were wrong about me. It's that they were confident, and specific, and in a position of authority, and still wrong — which is why I'm careful about saying that kind of thing to anyone who works for me.
