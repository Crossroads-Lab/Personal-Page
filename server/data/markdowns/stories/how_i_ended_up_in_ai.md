# How I ended up in AI

## How did you get into AI? What made you choose this field?

By mistake, and I mean that literally. I did not choose AI. AI was what was left.

I came to Oregon State in 2004 as a French exchange student, and I chose it because it was the closest school on my shortlist to the ocean. I wanted to surf. I meant to stay a year and stayed seven.

What I actually wanted to do was computer graphics — building technology for animation and visual effects studios. That was the dream, not machine learning.

## Is it true you were rejected from AI before you ended up in it?

Yes, and it's my favourite detail of the whole thing.

While I was looking for a mentor, a friend kept pushing me to join his professor, who worked in AI. I wasn't especially interested, but I went and interviewed. I was rejected on the spot — not for any reason I could follow, just that I wasn't a fit for it.

So I went and did graphics instead. Then my mentor didn't get tenure, and I was suddenly a student without an advisor. The professors who fished me out were AI professors. The choice was do AI or don't finish the PhD.

I did AI against my will, having been told a couple of years earlier that I wasn't suited to it. I ended up with Professor Sinisa Todorovic, as his first doctoral student, and I ended up enjoying the work enormously. It became 3D reconstruction, spatiotemporal video segmentation and event recognition, and that turned into the rest of my career.

I try to remember that whenever I'm about to tell someone confidently what they're not cut out for.

## What did you actually want to do instead?

Animation and special effects. Not making the films — building the technology the studios use to make them. I was a Pixar fan and that was the whole ambition.

I got close enough to touch it: I interned at Laika, the stop-motion studio in Oregon. That was the industry I thought I was heading into.

## Why did you stay at Oregon State after the exchange year?

I went to a presentation by the Dean of Engineering and decided I wanted to stay on for a master's. One problem: I had no intention of paying the tuition.

The way to avoid that was an assistantship, which meant two things — strong grades, and a professor willing to take me on. So I went and found both.

I finished in the top 1% at the college level and was elected to the Phi Kappa Phi Honors Society for it. That's the part I'm still a little proud of, mostly because the motivation behind it was so unglamorous: I just didn't want to pay tuition. The assistantship mattered more to me at the time than the honour did.

## Is it true you never meant to do a PhD?

Completely true. I never wanted to do a PhD. I hated writing.

What happened is that the student office gave me tactical advice: enrol on the PhD track, because that maximises your odds of getting the assistantship, and then just drop out at the master's level once you have it. Reasonable plan.

Then the program turned out to be genuinely good, the buildings were new, the work got interesting, and I never dropped. One more year became five more years.

For the full picture of how little I saw this coming: a friend back in France told me he wanted to do a PhD, and I called him nuts. I said I'd never do that. And here we are.

## What did your family make of you staying in the US?

This is my favourite part. Every year my mum asked when I was coming back to France, and every year I told her: next year.

Eventually I had to tell her I thought I'd be staying longer. She asked how much longer. I said much longer — ten, twenty years.

She gasped. I'm still here.

## Was AI an obvious career choice at the time?

Not remotely. This was the mid-2000s. The commercial applications you could point to were early search engines and basic face detection. There was no industry waiting for us.

We were a bunch of nerds who loved doing math. What kept us going was that we loved seeing the machine doing things that were completely sci-fi. That was the whole payoff — it certainly wasn't a career plan.

What I didn't see coming is that the expertise turned out to be in demand exactly when the wave hit. That was timing, not foresight.

## What do you tell students going into this field now?

Learn the foundations properly. Use AI as an aid, not a replacement for understanding — if you can't reason about what the system is doing, you can't tell when it's wrong. Think for yourself, think critically, and be resilient.

And I'd add this: having an "AI company" will soon sound as redundant as claiming to have an "internet company."
