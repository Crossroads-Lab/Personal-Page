# How I became a product manager

## How did you move from engineering into product?

I didn't move. I wrote myself the job because nobody else would do it.

At Amazon A9 I was a researcher, and I was blocked. To connect visual data to models I needed infrastructure that didn't exist — somewhere to store and manage very specialised data, and a way to retrain at the click of a button. Without it I was hand-rolling the same plumbing for every experiment.

I took it to the product team. Not their roadmap. I took it to the engineering team. Not their priority. Both answers were reasonable and both left me stuck.

So I wrote the whole thing myself: the product case, the architecture, the proposal, the timeline. Everything a PM would have produced, except there was no PM.

## What happened when you took that proposal around?

I went back to the product team and had them vet it properly. They read it and said yes.

Then I took it to engineering. By that point I could say, truthfully, that product had vetted and approved it. I did not go out of my way to correct anyone's assumption about where it had originated. Engineering reviewed the approach and the timeline and said yes too.

I'll own the shape of that: the vetting was real, the approval was real, and I let the provenance stay comfortably vague. It worked because the proposal was good, not because the routing was clever — but the routing is what got it read.

I became the lead on the project. That work became the global visual search database and the annotation and evaluation platform, running a corpus of over 12 million images and labels.

## So how did the title actually change?

About a month later the product team summoned me. I assumed I was in trouble.

They weren't annoyed — they liked the work and wanted me to join them. I said yes. That's how a researcher became a technical product manager, with no plan and no interview.

## Did you try to go back to engineering afterwards?

I did, and it lasted three days.

When I joined Snap I interviewed on the research and engineering side and passed it as such. Three days in, my boss asked me to switch hats — back to product. I ended up as Technical Program Manager and Senior Research Scientist, which is a title that exists precisely because I kept trying to be both.

Sometimes you can't escape your path.
