# Letting someone go at Heali

## Tell me about a time you had to manage a layoff or let someone go

At Heali we had to let go a senior leader, and I still think of it as my mistake rather than a failure on the other side of the table.

The hire was genuinely excellent — sharp, experienced, well regarded, and out of corporate consulting. What a seed-stage startup actually needed was someone scrappy, creative and fast. Those are different jobs wearing the same title. This wasn't someone failing at the job of being excellent. It was someone hired into a role that wanted a different person.

## How did you handle the human side of it?

The decision landed just before Christmas. I pushed to delay it until the middle of January, and I got that.

Nobody should take that call in the week before the holidays if the business can survive four more weeks — and ours could. It cost us something to wait. It was worth it. That meant going into the new year able to run a search properly rather than spending the break absorbing the news.

The bounce-back was quick, which I was relieved about and also slightly humbled by. The market agreed with my assessment of the person; it just hadn't agreed with my assessment of the fit.

## Whose fault was it?

Mine, at the interview. This is the clearest example I have of the thing I believe generally: a failed employee often comes with failed leadership.

We evaluated on capability, track record and credentials, and the candidate was strong on all three. We did not seriously evaluate whether the way this person worked matched the way a company at our stage has to work. That's not a question you can skip and then discover later at someone else's expense.

## What did you change afterwards?

Our hiring process. We built cultural-add evaluation into the interview properly instead of treating it as a feeling you form afterwards — explicit questions about how someone operates with no process, no support function, and no clear brief, because that was the actual job.

The cost of getting that wrong isn't a bad quarter. It's someone rearranging their life for a role that was never going to fit them.
