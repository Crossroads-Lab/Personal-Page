# Shipping past politics at YouTube

## Tell me about a time you faced organizational politics

At YouTube I had an idea for large-scale video quality detection and correction — the project that became Suggested Edits. Politics killed it before it started. It wasn't a technical objection; the idea just didn't survive the room.

## How have you made a controversial or challenging decision?

I hacked on it nights, cracked the hard part, and demoed a working version. Once there was something to look at instead of something to argue about, the conversation changed. It shipped across the entire YouTube video corpus, reaching 94M+ daily active users.

## Tell me about disagreeing with your manager or senior leadership

This is that story, from the other side of the table. I was the junior person in the room and leadership had decided the idea wasn't going anywhere.

I didn't escalate it, and I didn't keep relitigating it in meetings — I'd already lost that argument and repeating it would only have made me the person who couldn't let go. I disagreed by building the thing on my own time and coming back with something working.

That's a specific choice about how to push back: when the objection isn't technical, more argument doesn't move it. Evidence does. I'd rather absorb the cost of being wrong privately than spend the team's political capital on a debate I can settle by myself.

## What did you learn about pushing an idea through?

Prove it, don't debate it. A demo settles arguments that a document never will.

I'd add the caveat I only understood later: this works when you're right, and you don't know you're right yet. It's a bet on your own judgement that you fund personally — nights, weekends, no cover if it fails. That's a reasonable bet to take occasionally and a terrible way to operate continuously.

## Did it change anything for you afterwards?

It shipped, and then I left. Not in anger — but the experience told me something about where my ideas would get heard, and I moved to Amazon shortly after.

That's worth saying plainly: winning the argument by demo got the project built, and it did not change the environment that had killed it in the first place. If you keep having to route around the same wall, the wall is the information.

## What would you do differently? What do you regret?

I'd pair the conviction with market validation. Building it in the dark worked that time, but I was lucky that the thing I was sure about happened to be the thing users needed. I've gotten wiser about testing that assumption before spending the nights.
