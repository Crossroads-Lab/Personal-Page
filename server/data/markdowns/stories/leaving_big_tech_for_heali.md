# Why I left big tech

## Why did you leave big tech? What made you go found a company?

About a decade in — Google, then A9 at Amazon, then Snap — I wanted work that felt more direct. The scale was extraordinary and the problems were real, but the distance between what I built and who it helped kept growing.

The thing that actually moved me was noticing how little the math changed. An algorithm built to recommend a show someone is likely to want to watch is not far from one that can predict what someone with a chronic illness should be eating. Same shape of problem. Wildly different stakes.

That's what led to co-founding Heali in 2019.

## Was there a specific moment you decided?

There was, and it was a wall.

We'd just moved into the new Snap corporate building in Santa Monica. I was on my way to a meeting with our CTO and I walked past a freshly painted feature wall — bright yellow, black Snap logo, and a motto along the lines of *Snap brings joy*.

I stopped, and two objections arrived at once.

The first was professional: that's not a metric. Joy isn't observable, it isn't instrumented, and you cannot tell whether you're succeeding or failing at it. I've never had patience for a goal you can't be wrong about. If the measure of your work is a feeling you've asserted, you've given yourself permission to never check.

The second was harder, because I'd been building the thing for three years: I don't believe social media brings joy. Engagement, yes. Attention, certainly — I was good at producing it. Joy is a different claim, and I didn't think the evidence supported it.

That was the moment I decided to point the same skills at something where the benefit wasn't in question.

## So the objection was about measurement as much as ethics?

They turned out to be the same objection, which I didn't expect.

An unmeasurable goal and an unexamined one look identical from the inside. "We bring joy" can't be falsified, so nobody has to find out. Whereas at Heali, the question was whether someone's symptoms improved — uncomfortable, specific, and answerable. You can fail at that in public.

I'd rather work on something that can tell me I'm wrong.

## Was it hard to walk away from that kind of scale?

The reach was the hardest part to give up. At Snap I shipped machine learning for augmented reality and friend recommendations that served hundreds of millions of people, and you don't replace that number at a seed-stage company.

What you get instead is that every decision is yours and the feedback is immediate. At a certain point I'd rather be close enough to the problem to feel it.

## What did you take from big tech into founding?

The engineering standards, mostly — how to build something that survives real traffic, and what a serious review process looks like. What I had to unlearn was the assumption that there's a platform underneath you. At Heali we were the platform, and nobody was coming to fix it.

That gap is most of why I started Crossroads Venture Studio in 2023: giving technical founders the operational insight I wish I'd had when I started Heali.
